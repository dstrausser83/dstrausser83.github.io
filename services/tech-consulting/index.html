The Technology Decisions Small Businesses Get Wrong

Small businesses don’t have a technology problem. They have a technology-decision problem. Same budget, wildly different outcomes — here’s what separates them.

Mistake one: buying software before fixing the process

This is the big one, and I’ve watched it play out for over a decade in the ERP world. A company has a messy process — orders get lost, inventory counts are fiction, nobody knows the real margins — and the answer is always “we need new software.”

Here’s the problem: software doesn’t fix processes. It amplifies them. Automate a mess and you get a faster mess — now with monthly subscription fees. The order of operations matters: understand the process, simplify it, then pick the tool that fits the simplified version. Do it backwards and you’ll spend good money digitizing your dysfunction.

Mistake two: the shiny-tool graveyard

Every small business has one: a stack of subscriptions nobody fully uses. The project tool from 2022. The marketing platform someone bought after a webinar. The “AI solution” that’s really just a chatbot with a logo.

The fix is a ruthless annual audit. List every tool, what it costs, who actually uses it, and what would break if you cancelled it tomorrow. You’ll be shocked how many answers are “nobody,” “too much,” and “nothing.” Cancel with confidence — that’s found money for the tools that actually matter.

Mistake three: best-of-breed sprawl

There’s a tool that’s “best” at everything — best CRM, best accounting, best inventory, best email. Buy them all and congratulations: you’ve built a full-time job called keeping twelve systems in sync. Your team spends their day doing swivel-chair work, copying data from one screen to another, and nobody trusts any of the numbers because they never match.

For most small businesses, one integrated system that’s good enough at everything beats five perfect tools that don’t talk to each other. Integration isn’t a feature — it’s the whole point. Every disconnected system is a tax on your team’s time and your data’s accuracy.

Mistake four: ignoring the data you already have

Small business owners make huge decisions — hiring, pricing, inventory, expansion — on gut feel, while sitting on data that could answer the question. Not because they’re careless; because the data is trapped in five systems that don’t agree with each other (see mistake three).

You don’t need a data warehouse or a dashboard consultant. You need one trustworthy source for the numbers that run the business: real margins, real cash position, real pipeline. Get that right and gut feel becomes informed gut feel — which is how good owners have always operated.

Mistake five: treating security as an afterthought

Nobody thinks about security until the ransomware note shows up. The basics aren’t expensive or complicated: real backups that get tested, multi-factor authentication on everything, and access that matches people’s actual jobs — the intern doesn’t need the bank login.

Small businesses are targets because they assume they aren’t. The fix costs less than one bad week.

Mistake six: forgetting the total cost

The license is the cheapest part of any technology decision. The real costs are implementation, data migration, training, the productivity dip while everyone learns the new system, and the ongoing care and feeding. A “cheap” tool that needs a consultant to keep it running isn’t cheap.

Always price the whole thing: buy it, install it, learn it, run it for three years. Then compare. The sticker price is marketing; the total cost is the truth.

Frequently asked questions

Should we build custom software or buy off the shelf?

Buy, almost always. Custom software is a second business you didn’t ask to run — you own the bugs, the updates, and the developer who might leave. Buy the thing that’s close enough, configure it to fit, and save custom development for the one or two processes that are genuinely your competitive edge.

Cloud or on-premise — which is right for us?

For most small businesses, cloud wins: no servers to babysit, updates handled for you, access from anywhere. On-premise still makes sense in specific cases — certain compliance needs, unreliable connectivity, or existing infrastructure you’ve already paid for. We’ll look at your situation, not the trend.

How do I pick a technology consultant I can trust?

Watch for the tell: if they recommend a product in the first meeting, they’re a salesperson. A real consultant starts with your processes and your problems, and the technology recommendation comes after the diagnosis. Ask who they don’t recommend and why — the answer tells you everything.
