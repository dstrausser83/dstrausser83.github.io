What Playing Guitar Taught Me About Running a Business

2026-09-22 · by David Strausser

Quick answer

Playing guitar taught me that mastery comes from deliberate practice, timing beats speed, listening matters more than showing off, and you get better fastest by playing with people better than you. Every one of those applies directly to running a business.

I've been playing guitar for decades — in bands, on stages, in living rooms at 1 AM. Somewhere along the way I realized the instrument was quietly teaching me how to run a business. Here's the setlist.

1. Practice doesn't make perfect — perfect practice does

Strumming the same three chords for ten years makes you a ten-year beginner. Growth comes from deliberate practice: the uncomfortable stuff, the techniques you avoid, the metronome work nobody finds fun. Business is identical. Doing the same comfortable tasks for a decade doesn't build a company — attacking the hard things (sales calls, difficult conversations, learning the numbers) does.

2. Timing beats speed

Every young guitarist wants to play fast. Every mature one learns that playing in time — locking with the drummer, serving the song — matters infinitely more. In business, the equivalent is execution rhythm: consistent follow-up beats frantic hustle. The company that ships on time every time beats the one that sprints and stalls.

3. Listen more than you play

The best musicians in any room are the best listeners. They hear what the song needs and play exactly that — sometimes that's a blazing solo, sometimes it's two notes. Leaders who listen — to customers, to employees, to the market — make better decisions than the ones always waiting for their turn to talk. Your customers are telling you what to build. Are you listening or soloing?

4. Play with people better than you

Nothing accelerates your growth like being the worst player in the band. It's humbling and it's the fastest education in music. Same in business: hire people smarter than you, partner with people further along, join rooms where you're the rookie. Ego is the enemy of growth — in bands and in boardrooms.

5. Gear doesn't make the player

Beginners obsess over equipment. Pros can make a cheap guitar sing. In business, this is the "we need better software / more funding / a bigger team" trap. Tools help, but they don't replace skill, judgment, and reps. I've seen companies with million-dollar tech stacks get outplayed by a hungry team with spreadsheets and hustle. Master the fundamentals first.

6. The audience doesn't care about your excuses

Nobody at the show cares that your strings are old or you didn't sleep well. They paid for a performance. Your customers are the same — they don't care about your supply chain problems or your staffing issues. They care about what you deliver. Professionals handle the chaos backstage and deliver on stage.

Frequently asked questions

Do you still play guitar?

Every chance I get. It's still the best thinking time I have — half my business ideas arrive somewhere between the second verse and the guitar solo.

What's your band?

I play with Torment Ridden — we released "Molech (Valley of Slaughter)," an anti-murder track with a message that matters. Music with something to say beats background noise every time.

How does music actually help your business career?

Pattern recognition, performance under pressure, teamwork, and creativity on demand. Also: there's no faster way to bond with a client than finding out you both play.
